# Rite-controlled LAN Time Service options

Research date: 2026-07-27. Sources are limited to protocol specifications, official project documentation, operating-system and hypervisor documentation, and first-party vendor documentation.

## Recommendation

Make `time.fearn.cloud` a Rite-controlled **NTPv4 service endpoint**, resolved by LAN DNS while the WAN is unavailable. Initially host Chrony on a small, dedicated physical Linux system outside Proxmox and every Cluster. Discipline it from at least four independent, diverse, authenticated Internet sources when the WAN is present, persist drift/RTC data, and deliberately serve a high-stratum local reference during WAN loss. Add GNSS with PPS if the required WAN-outage duration and maximum clock error cannot be met by measured free-running holdover.

Do not make a VM the sole time authority, and do not put the service on a Proxmox host. A VM is adequate as a secondary server but inherits host timekeeping, scheduling, boot-order, and availability dependencies. A Proxmox-host service creates a circular recovery dependency: the host needs correct time, but the named service disappears whenever that same host is being recovered. Proxmox itself says its cluster stack relies heavily on precisely synchronized clocks and directs sites without public Internet access to local NTP servers. ([Proxmox VE Administration Guide](https://pve.proxmox.com/pve-docs/pve-admin-guide.pdf))

If the accepted error must stay below roughly a second over multi-day WAN outages, or the service must cold-start accurately with both WAN and Proxmox absent, skip the software-only first stage and buy or build a GNSS/PPS-backed physical server. A commercial appliance with OCXO or rubidium holdover buys quantified behavior and reduced integration work; a small Linux system plus a PPS-capable receiver and Chrony buys most of the independence at lower cost but leaves qualification, antenna, oscillator, monitoring, and lifecycle engineering to Rite.

## What the endpoint promises

The endpoint should promise **availability and bounded error**, not simply “NTP responds.” These are different service states:

1. **Disciplined:** upstream authenticated sources or a local hardware reference are selected and the service is traceable to UTC.
2. **Holdover:** the service has lost its references but continues from its learned oscillator frequency; clients remain mutually synchronized, while uncertainty from UTC grows.
3. **Unqualified:** the service has cold-started without a trustworthy reference or exceeded its allowed holdover error. It must not silently claim trustworthy UTC.

Chrony's `local` directive deliberately makes a server appear synchronized to clients even when it was never synchronized or its last update was long ago; its documented purpose is keeping an isolated network mutually synchronized, not guaranteeing real time. The `activate`, `distance`, `waitsynced`, and `waitunsynced` options can prevent premature activation, and orphan mode coordinates multiple local servers. ([chrony configuration: local reference](https://chrony-project.org/doc/4.8/chrony.conf.html#local)) Therefore Canon must define a maximum outage/error budget and Rite must expose the current state and uncertainty separately from the NTP answer.

Chrony estimates clock frequency from past measurements and free-runs using its best estimate after going offline. It can also track RTC error and restore its estimate across power cycles when configured to persist histories and RTC data. ([chrony configuration: computers with infrequent connections](https://chrony-project.org/doc/4.8/chrony.conf.html#_computers_with_infrequent_connections_to_ntp_servers)) A configured `maxclockerror` expresses assumed oscillator instability; Chrony gives 10 ppm as an example for a low-quality clock and 0.1 ppm for a high-quality temperature-compensated oscillator. ([chrony configuration: `maxclockerror`](https://chrony-project.org/doc/4.8/chrony.conf.html#maxclockerror)) By arithmetic, 1 ppm permits 86.4 ms/day of additional error, 10 ppm 864 ms/day, and 100 ppm 8.64 s/day. Those are planning bounds, not measurements of a particular machine.

## Architecture comparison

| Option | Acquisition and WAN-loss behavior | Independence and failure shape | Trust, compatibility, and operational cost | Judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Existing network edge/router service** | Usually disciplines its ordinary system clock from upstream NTP, then free-runs during WAN loss. A router with a supported GNSS/PTP input is a different, stronger design. Cold-start correctness depends on its RTC and firmware behavior. pfSense is evidence that this option can be real: its official NTP server supports access restrictions and an orphan mode that uses the local clock when other clocks are unavailable, and it can consume serial GPS/PPS. ([pfSense NTP server](https://docs.netgate.com/pfsense/en/latest/services/ntpd/server.html), [pfSense serial GPS](https://docs.netgate.com/pfsense/en/latest/services/ntpd/gps.html)) | Strongly independent of Proxmox and Clusters, already always on, and naturally reachable across LANs. A single edge device is also a shared failure domain with routing and LAN DNS; firmware upgrades/reboots can remove all three together. | Plain NTP is broadly compatible, but platform-specific evidence is mandatory: confirm it can serve while unsynchronized, report state/error, restrict clients, persist drift, and accept multiple authenticated sources. Appliance-style router UIs often expose fewer controls than Chrony. | **Good only after model-specific qualification.** Prefer as a secondary source, or primary when the edge platform demonstrably implements the full readiness contract. |
| **Dedicated GNSS-backed appliance** | GNSS makes it an NTP primary server independent of WAN. Oscillator grade determines GNSS-loss holdover. As one concrete upper-end example, Microchip specifies optional OCXO holdover of 25 microseconds for the first day and rubidium holdover below 1 microsecond for 24 hours and below 3 microseconds after three days on the SyncServer S600. ([S600 data sheet](https://ww1.microchip.com/downloads/aemdocuments/documents/ftd/productdocuments/brochures/syncserver-s600-data-sheet-2903.pdf)) Its guide describes stopping NTP reference updates at a configured holdover-error limit and falling back to other NTP servers. ([S600/S650 user guide](https://ww1.microchip.com/downloads/aemDocuments/documents/FTD/ProductDocuments/UserGuides/SyncServer-S600-S650-User-Guide-3865D.pdf)) | Independent of WAN, Proxmox, and Clusters; can cold-start from GNSS. Still depends on power, antenna/sky view, receiver health, and one chassis unless duplicated. GNSS is a local external input, not automatically trustworthy against jamming, spoofing, receiver faults, or rollover. | Highest acquisition cost but clearest vendor-supported holdover envelope. Products vary in NTS support, licensing, management, and oscillator option, so procurement must verify the exact SKU. | **Best when error is strict or outages are long.** Probably excessive if Canon only needs recovery systems within seconds for short outages. |
| **Dedicated physical Linux + Chrony** | Uses several NTS-authenticated Internet sources while online; on WAN loss, Chrony can activate a high-stratum local reference and free-run from learned drift. A PPS-capable GNSS receiver can make it a local primary source. Chrony supports GPS/reference clocks, PPS, NTS client/server, and hardware packet timestamping. ([chronyd manual](https://chrony-project.org/doc/4.8/chronyd.html), [chrony installation features](https://chrony-project.org/doc/4.8/installation.html), [Chrony hardware timestamping](https://chrony-project.org/doc/4.8/chrony.conf.html#hwtimestamp)) | Independent of Proxmox and Clusters and cheap to replace. Commodity oscillator/RTC behavior is not specified, and a single box remains a failure point. Adding a PPS receiver requires direct hardware access, good antenna placement, and validation. PPS supplies precision but also needs a source that numbers the seconds. ([NTPsec PPS documentation](https://docs.ntpsec.org/latest/pps.html)) | Full Chrony controls, metrics, access restrictions, and NTS. Rite owns OS patching, configuration, RTC/drift persistence, hardware qualification, and replacement. | **Recommended baseline.** It is the cleanest balance of independence, inspectability, compatibility, and cost; add GNSS/PPS based on the measured holdover requirement. |
| **Dedicated Chrony VM** | Same software acquisition and local-reference behavior as physical Linux, but its free-running clock is virtual. It has no independent oscillator comparable to a physical RTC/TCXO unless hardware is passed through. | Independent of a Cluster if it is a separate Rite VM, but not independent of Proxmox, the selected host, host clock, VM boot order, snapshots, pauses, or migration. Linux documents that virtual time is complicated by host scheduling/preemption, delayed interrupts, TSC behavior, and migration between machines with different clock rates. ([Linux KVM timekeeping](https://docs.kernel.org/virt/kvm/x86/timekeeping.html)) | Easy to manage and replicate. Chrony's own security guidance notes that much larger drift is possible in some VMs and that migration can change clock-source calibration. ([chrony FAQ: system-clock security](https://chrony-project.org/faq.html#_how_can_i_make_the_system_clock_more_secure)) | **Secondary only.** Suitable for service redundancy after a physical authority exists, not as the sole recovery time source. |
| **Chrony on a Proxmox host** | The host already uses Chrony in current Proxmox VE and has a physical clock; enabling server access and holdover is mechanically small. ([Proxmox VE Administration Guide](https://pve.proxmox.com/pve-docs/pve-admin-guide.pdf), [chrony FAQ: making a server](https://chrony-project.org/faq.html#_how_do_i_make_an_ntp_server)) | Avoids VM clock caveats and extra hardware, but couples a recovery prerequisite to the hypervisor being recovered. Maintenance, reinstall, security hardening, or failure of that host removes time. In a multi-host installation each host would need a deliberate redundant design. | Expands the network-facing role and configuration of a critical hypervisor. It also complicates using `time.fearn.cloud` as that host's own independent source and risks package/config divergence from supported Proxmox defaults. | **Reject as the sole endpoint.** It may be a downstream/secondary server, but its marginal savings are not worth the circular dependency. |
| **Two or more independent Chrony servers in orphan mode** | Each normally uses external or hardware references. When all real sources disappear, Chrony's orphan mode elects one local server and lets the others synchronize to it; another takes over if the elected server fails. ([chrony configuration: orphan mode](https://chrony-project.org/doc/4.8/chrony.conf.html#local)) | Removes the single-server failure and preserves one LAN time domain. It does not create UTC accuracy: all members can share the same wrong time, and common upstream, power, DNS, or GNSS failures remain. | Clients should be configured to poll multiple actual addresses. One DNS name with multiple addresses is not sufficient evidence that each client implementation will sample all servers: Chrony documents `pool` for names representing multiple servers, while each configured `server` uses one address at a time. ([chrony configuration: `server` and `pool`](https://chrony-project.org/doc/4.8/chrony.conf.html#server)) | **Best later redundancy pattern.** Use two failure-independent physical authorities and expose their identities as well as the stable service name; do not hide the topology behind untested DNS round-robin behavior. |

## Time acquisition and source selection

For a software-based service, configure multiple independent upstreams, preferably NTS-authenticated and from different operators. Chrony recommends at least three servers for reliable operation and explains that multiple agreeing sources plus `minsources` make selection more robust against one false source. IETF's NTP Best Current Practice goes further: operators concerned with accurate time should use at least four independent, diverse sources, so three remain after one fails. ([chrony FAQ: minimum client configuration](https://chrony-project.org/faq.html#_what_is_the_minimum_recommended_configuration_for_an_ntp_client), [chrony FAQ: securing the system clock](https://chrony-project.org/faq.html#_how_can_i_make_the_system_clock_more_secure), [RFC 8633, Section 3.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8633.html#section-3.2)) NTP's selection algorithm seeks a majority clique of presumed correct candidates and refuses to discipline the clock when it cannot find enough survivors. ([RFC 5905, Section 11.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5905.html#section-11.2))

GNSS plus PPS materially changes the service from WAN-held secondary time to a local primary time source. NTP defines a primary server as directly synchronized to a reference clock traceable to UTC, such as GPS or Galileo. ([RFC 5905, Section 2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5905.html#section-2)) PPS improves precision to a few microseconds on conventional systems, but it does not identify which second the pulse represents; another source must supply the seconds numbering. ([NTPsec PPS documentation](https://docs.ntpsec.org/latest/pps.html)) A GNSS design should still compare against independent network sources while WAN is available and alarm on disagreement rather than automatically trusting one receiver.

## Authentication and LAN trust limits

Ordinary NTP over UDP 123 does not authenticate `time.fearn.cloud` merely because clients reached it through trusted DNS or a controlled VLAN. Chrony's threat analysis says an on-path attacker can forge, modify, delay, drop, or redirect responses and can manipulate both time and frequency. NTS or symmetric keys prevent forged/modified/redirected packets, but delay and denial attacks remain possible and require monitoring and bounds. ([chrony FAQ: securing the system clock](https://chrony-project.org/faq.html#_how_can_i_make_the_system_clock_more_secure))

NTS is the preferred authenticated mode for Chrony-capable clients. RFC 8915 uses TLS 1.3 on TCP 4460 for key establishment, followed by authenticated NTPv4 messages and client-held cookies over UDP 123; it secures client/server mode and protects the client from replay. ([RFC 8915, Sections 1.3 and 3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8915.html#section-1.3)) Chrony can serve NTS when configured with a certificate and key, and can persist cookie-encryption keys so clients retain working cookies across a server restart. ([chrony configuration: NTS server](https://chrony-project.org/doc/4.8/chrony.conf.html#ntsservercert), [Ubuntu Chrony NTS server guide](https://ubuntu.com/server/docs/how-to/networking/serve-ntp-with-chrony/))

NTS does not remove offline bootstrap dependencies. During WAN loss, LAN DNS must still resolve `time.fearn.cloud`; clients must trust the issuing CA; the certificate must be valid at the client's current clock; and Rite must be able to renew/deploy it without the WAN or a Cluster if the outage model demands that. Persisted NTS cookies reduce repeat key establishment but are not a universal recovery mechanism. This is an architectural inference from NTS's TLS/certificate exchange and Chrony's persisted-cookie behavior.

Serve plain NTP in parallel on restricted recovery and infrastructure VLANs because common clients do not all support NTS. Linux clients using Chrony can use NTS, while Windows' built-in W32Time accepts ordinary NTP/SNTP and manually named peers; Microsoft explicitly says manually specified sources outside the domain hierarchy are unauthenticated and vulnerable. ([Windows Time service operation](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/networking/windows-time-service/how-the-windows-time-service-works), [Windows non-Windows NTP interoperability](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/troubleshoot/windows-server/active-directory/time-synchronization-not-succeed-non-ntp)) Network ACLs reduce exposure but are not cryptographic server authentication. Symmetric NTP MAC keys are possible, but Chrony recommends a unique key per client/server relationship, creating secret-distribution and client-compatibility cost. ([chrony FAQ: securing the system clock](https://chrony-project.org/faq.html#_how_can_i_make_the_system_clock_more_secure))

## Redundancy and endpoint naming

`time.fearn.cloud` should be a stable External Service Endpoint, but its DNS record is also part of the recovery substrate. LAN DNS must authoritatively resolve it without recursive Internet access, with an address reachable from Recovery Workstations, Proxmox hosts, and Cluster VMs before any Cluster exists. Keep the concrete server identities (for example `time-a` and `time-b`) separately resolvable for monitoring and for clients that can sample multiple sources.

A single service name can initially map to one physical host. If redundancy is added, prefer multiple explicitly configured server names or a client `pool` configuration over assuming multiple A/AAAA records automatically provide sampling and failover. DHCP option 42 can distribute an ordered list of NTP server **IP addresses**, not a hostname, to clients that consume it. ([RFC 2132, Section 8.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2132.html#section-8.3)) Chrony's reliable-client recommendation is at least three sources; IETF recommends four for accurate service. Two disagreeing clocks cannot identify which is correct, so two nodes provide availability but not majority-based correctness. ([chrony FAQ: minimum configuration](https://chrony-project.org/faq.html#_what_is_the_minimum_recommended_configuration_for_an_ntp_client), [RFC 8633, Section 3.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8633.html#section-3.2))

## Required WAN-disabled readiness evidence

Rite should produce evidence from a scheduled drill, not infer readiness from configuration. With WAN egress physically or administratively blocked and no Cluster services available:

1. Resolve `time.fearn.cloud` through the same LAN DNS path a clean Recovery Workstation and a newly created Cluster VM will use.
2. Cold-boot the time server and at least one clean Linux client. Show whether the service enters **disciplined**, **holdover**, or **unqualified** state; a cold boot from RTC is distinct from continuous powered holdover.
3. From each required network segment, verify UDP 123 and, for an NTS client, TCP 4460 plus authenticated UDP 123. Verify that unauthorized segments are denied.
4. Demonstrate plain NTP compatibility for recovery clients that lack NTS and NTS authentication for a Chrony client, including certificate validation using only offline-available DNS, trust roots, and credentials.
5. Record source reachability, selected source/reference ID, stratum, system offset, frequency estimate, skew, root delay/dispersion, and server state at WAN cutoff and throughout the maximum promised outage.
6. Compare against an independent reference that was not used to discipline the server during the test. Require observed error to remain inside a predeclared bound; “clients agree” is insufficient because a local reference is explicitly allowed to be wrong relative to UTC.
7. Reboot or fail the active server during the outage. If redundancy is claimed, demonstrate client convergence on the surviving concrete address and show that the stable endpoint still works.
8. Restore WAN access and show controlled reselection and convergence without an unsafe clock step after recovery-sensitive services have started.
9. Repeat after the maximum planned powered-off interval, certificate age, and configuration restore from Rite's source of truth. This tests RTC recovery, offline certificate/trust availability, and reproducibility rather than only runtime holdover.

## Decisions this research exposes

1. Set the maximum WAN outage and powered-off interval, plus the maximum acceptable error from UTC at each interval. These decide whether commodity holdover is enough or GNSS/TCXO/OCXO is required.
2. Decide whether the first implementation is one dedicated physical Chrony host or two failure-independent hosts in orphan mode.
3. Decide whether GNSS jamming/spoofing is in the threat model and, if so, what independent source comparison and alarm policy prevents a lone receiver from becoming unquestioned authority.
4. Define the LAN DNS records and concrete server identities behind `time.fearn.cloud`, including how DNS itself is restored without time or a Cluster.
5. Inventory required clients and classify them as NTS-capable, symmetric-key capable, or plain-NTP-only; then define VLAN/firewall access and downgrade policy.
6. Define the offline certificate authority, renewal, deployment, and NTS cookie-key persistence needed for `time.fearn.cloud`.
7. Specify monitoring thresholds for source loss, holdover age, estimated error, oscillator frequency/skew, GNSS state, NTS failures, and service reachability, and retain that evidence outside all Clusters.
